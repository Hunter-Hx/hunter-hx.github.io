Essay: Data Protection And Governance

Future of Privacy in Kenya: Is it possible to be invisible in a data-driven world?

In the digital age, where large amounts of personal data are collected, used and stored every day, and as technology continues to advance, the protection of personal data becomes a great concern for individuals. From the government to private entities, there is one algorithm that consumes how people interact. While these advancements have increased efficiency and access to services, they have also raised concerns and questions about how our data is handled, used and to who specifically has access to the data behind our backs. With technology being more complex for over millions of Kenyans, a great question arises; is it possible to be invisible in the modern Kenya with data collection at the center of everyday life? This essay answers the question and exhausts what the Data Protection Act 2019 which was enacted to; to regulate the processing of personal data; (b) to ensure that the processing of personal data of a data subject is guided by the principles set out in section 25; (c) to protect the privacy of individuals; (d) to establish the legal and institutional mechanism to protect personal data; and the section 31 of the Constitution of the Republic of Kenya verses the reality and the hypothesis behind the mask which goes behind what data protection is and far beyond the principles of data protection. But what is data protection? It is the process of safeguarding information from unauthorized access, theft, or corruption.

Data protection Vs Surveillance pressures.

The right to privacy is a fundamental human right enshrined in international laws reflecting its universal recognition and significance. Internationally, it is provided under Article 12 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and reaffirmed in Article 17 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights, which prohibits arbitrary interference with one’s privacy, family, home, or correspondence. This right obligates state parties to respect and ensure the protection of personal autonomy and dignity. Kenya has domesticated these laws under Article 2(5) and (6) of the Constitution, forming part of the laws of Kenya. Kenya enacted the Data protection Act of 2019, a modern law that was intended to protect personal data from collection and processing and even sharing. However, the enforcements of these rights have remained limited. Many citizens are still unaware of their privacy rights, and the enforcement bodies like the Office of the Data Protection Commissioner face constraints in resources and reach. Despite Kenya’s effort and progress in protecting citizens personal data, persistent challenges have weakened the system from within and the greatest challenge has been lack of public awareness.
Millions of Kenyans have remained in the dark on how their data is used or even transferred. A great mistrust has emerged as surveillance has increased. On 15th October, 2025, President Ruto assented into law the Computer Misuse and Cybercrimes Act 2024. Over millions of Kenyans, up to date, have no idea of what that amendment represented. Critics argue that the law invaded privacy and restricted digital freedoms. While these might just remain as laws, Kenyans feel their privacy was greatly tampered with as the government enacted these laws. With the government and private entities at the center of it all, it is inconclusive to say one can be invisible in the modern Kenya. From mobile money transfers to banks to the new AI technology, it is near impossible to be invisible in Kenya… unless of course you are not using either government or private sectors.

Judicial Enforcement and Landmark Cases.

In mid-May 2023, a project that offered cryptocurrency in exchange of iris scan boomed in Kenya. Over 300,000 Kenyans registered for it. With no notion of how the collected data was going to be used, adding the fact that it was not Kenyan based movement, Kenyans simply gave in for the ‘free cash.’ The High Court ruled that the Worldcoin’s practices had violated the DPA and constitutional privacy rights due to its inadequate Data protection Impact assessment, unclear consent mechanisms, and improper cross-border data transfers. Biometric data, a class of data inherently sensitive because it is irreversible, should then be handled with care as per the DPA. The Worldcoin was then barred from its services in registering Kenyans but the damage is already caused. Not because Kenyans are incompetent but because the Office of the Data protection Commissioner failed to do its job. No sensitive information, like biometrics should be collected from citizens without the responsible body overseeing it. Just from the collected iris, one is not only traceable in Kenya but also everywhere in the world if the data is used other than its intended purpose. It was all public unawareness and office incompetency.

Mobile Money and Digital Identity Kenya’s global leadership in mobile money — notably M-Pesa — has normalized digital transactions involving personal data. Every transaction carries identifiers linking it to a user’s phone number, SIM registration, and often, identity documents. Privacy-preserving research is emerging (for instance, alternative protocols that minimize exposure of personal data in mobile money transactions), but such solutions are not mainstream. Thus, even routine economic activity mandates digital footprints that are difficult to erase. For most Kenyans, invisibility is effectively precluded by participation in everyday digital life.
Artificial Intelligence and Data Analytics Theres been a rapid development in AI over the past few years. Its advancements ha come with challenges that are far less conspicuous but the damage could be catastrophic. With ability to hold large data bases, they have been deployed in sectors like healthcare, finance, education and e-government. Efficiency is what humans want, to do something modernly without having to struggle to do it manually, but are they really reliable in keeping our data? The problem has always been not the system but the people handling the data on the system. Kenya’s DPA requires explicit consent for data processing, but lacks specific regulations tailored to AI risks — such as automated decision-making and profiling. But as we all know that the weakest link in every security chain has always been human, we can only hope that it doesn’t go haywire. For example, the AI-driven decision systems recruitment could perpetuate historical biases embedded in training data, leading to discriminatory outcomes. These impacts are often invisible until harm occurs, making privacy enforcement reactive rather than preventative, and complicating the notion of voluntary invisibility in data-rich environments. The purpose of the DPA is thus to prevent moment like that happening, instead, they have been doing the opposite; solving the problem they could have prevented.

Corporate Data Practices and Surveillance Capitalism The question of invisibility in a data driven world is clouded in predictable and unpredictable moments. Every system requires a data input, whether a name, Identification Number or phone number. Telecommunications companies, social media platforms and financial tech firms amass behavioral, locational and transactional data that can be monetized or shared with partners. While the DPA criminalizes unauthorized sale of personal data, enforcement remains inconsistent. And it the inconsistency (the solving and not the preventing) that leaves Kenyans in awe and mistrust in the system. Corporate surveillances often operate under opaque privacy policies. With the monitor’s assumptions that they are doing the right thing, it’s always a whisk whether the data is protected or left unprotected. Research indicates that many organizations fail to disclose data practices clearly, leaving consumers unaware of how their data is exploited. In that kind of environment, consent is unclear- people agree to complex terms without understanding consequences, eroding meaningful control over personal data. Institutional Realities: Enforcement and Awareness
Effective privacy protection depends not only on laws, but on institutions that can enforce them. The ODPC has made strides, including conferences on emerging privacy issues and regional office expansion, but enforcement remains constrained by resources and public engagement challenges. The regulator also issues guidance notes on sensitive data categories like biometrics, underscoring its evolving role, but compliance monitoring across all sectors is a Herculean task. Public awareness is another pressing challenge. Low awareness of privacy rights among Kenyans — especially rural and marginalized groups — dilutes the impact of legal protections. Without understanding their rights or how to report violations, citizens remain vulnerable to misuse of their personal data.

Is digital invisibility possible?
Given the current trajectory, digital invisibility for the average Kenyan is effectively unattainable. Several overlapping dynamics make this clear:
Technological Complexity- Emerging technologies like AI complicate consent and privacy in ways current laws are only beginning to grapple with.
Biometric Systems: Once enrolled, biometric identifiers ensure an individual’s data trail is persistent, interoperable and difficult to sever.
Weak Enforcement & Awareness: Legal protections exist but lack enforcement muscle and public understanding necessary to make privacy meaningful.
Pervasive Data Collection: Participation in essential services (mobile money, digital IDs, health systems) generates immutable digital footprints.
Paths Toward Stronger Privacy
While invisibility remains a lofty and largely unrealistic goal for most, several measures could strengthen privacy outcomes:
Stronger Enforcement: Empower the ODPC with greater resources, investigative capacity, and penalties to deter violations.
Public Education Campaigns: Nationwide campaigns on data rights and digital literacy could enable citizens to understand and assert their privacy rights.
Tailored AI Regulations: Develop specific frameworks addressing automated decision-making, transparency and bias mitigation mechanisms.
Privacy by Design Standards: Mandate that organizations build systems that minimize data collection and prioritize anonymization.
Cross-Sector Collaboration: Encourage partnerships between government, civil society and private sectors to align data practices with human rights.
These reforms would not make invisibility ubiquitous, but they could restore meaningful control over personal data and protect individuals from exploitation.
Conclusion In Kenya’s data-driven world, complete digital invisibility is an idealistic goal rather than a practical option for most citizens. Meaningful privacy protection demands robust legal enforcement, technological safeguards that limit unnecessary data exposure, and an empowered citizenry that understands and asserts their rights.
Kenya’s journey toward privacy resilience is underway, evidenced by judicial rulings upholding data protection principles and evolving regulatory guidance. Yet if privacy is to be more than a theoretical right, the state, corporations and civil society must move beyond mere compliance toward proactive protection in a landscape where data is both a resource and a vulnerability.
As total invisibility is unarchivable, prevention and protection are possible if we all play our cards right.
